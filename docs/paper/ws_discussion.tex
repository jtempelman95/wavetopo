\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What is established, and how firmly}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Mechanism.} Fiber orientation controls in-plane elastic waves by \emph{channeling} their energy
  along the fibers. The evidence is fourfold: the flux follows the fibers within a few degrees wherever they
  turn by less than about $4$\,rad per wavelength; fields that were never optimized channel just as
  tightly; rays traced through the fiber field alone predict the redirection to within about $20\%$; and
  redirection vanishes for isotropic material and grows with anisotropy. Channeling is a qP, ray-regime
  effect: it fails at long wavelengths, near sharply converging fibers and at standing-wave nodes, and
  about a quarter to a half of the lens's kinetic energy is not qP-like.
\item \textbf{Size.} With a converged absorbing layer the lens redirects $\WBandConvMin$--$\WBandConvMax\times$ more
  energy into its target, band-averaged over the tested wavelengths, and absorbs several times more of the
  injected power there. The larger values obtained with a reflecting layer ($\WRedirBandMean\times$) and at the
  design frequency ($\WGainForty\times$ in $G$) include reflections and resonance respectively.
\item \textbf{Limits.} The lever is bounded by the anisotropy and by the tow radius, with no loss while the
  minimum tow radius is below about $0.7$ along-fiber wavelengths and a gradual decline beyond (best designs
  found, lower bounds).
\item \textbf{Cancellation.} A full cloak must rely on interference, because exact cloaking needs anisotropy
  that no lamina has. Strong cancellation holds only near the design frequency and is tuned to the mesh it
  was optimized on; the curvature-limited design keeps a moderate $\WCloakCurvBandMin$--$\WCloakCurv\times$ across
  $\omega\in[40,60]$. The inextensible-fiber limit \cite{olsson2011} and ground-plane cloaks \cite{chen2011,popa2011}
  escape this bound.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Consequences for how wave-control designs are reported}
A finite, lightly damped plate driven at one frequency is a resonator, and an optimizer maximizing a field
energy at that frequency exploits the resonance whenever it can. We therefore reaffirm three practices,
established for wave topology optimization \cite{jensen2005,andkjaer2013}: report redirection (a share of
energy or of injected power) alongside, or instead of, raw gains; evaluate every design across a band and
against a converged absorbing layer; and optimize band-averaged objectives when the application has a band
(Sec.~\ref{sec:panel}).

\subsection{Relation to other orientation-design work}
Orientation fields with smoothness or curvature limits have been designed for compliance \cite{wong2025,jiang2024},
frequency \cite{abdalla2007,sheikhi2024,pagani2025} and, together with topology and microstructure, for
mechanical cloaking \cite{lutong2025}. What differs here is the target (time-harmonic routing of in-plane
elastic waves by a single lamina whose orientation is the only design field) and the analysis of energy
transport, which identifies the mechanism and separates it from the resonance of the finite plate.

\subsection{Limitations and scope}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Model.} A single plane-stress lamina, with no through-thickness or flexural waves and constant
  thickness. The as-built check (Sec.~\ref{sec:asbuilt}) is a continuum estimate of tow spreading and
  defects; tow width, process defects and multi-ply laminates are not resolved, so the designs are
  tow-curvature-constrained, not shown to be manufacturable.
\item \textbf{Absorbing layer.} The paper's layer reflects $|R|\approx\WReflMin$--$\WReflMax$. We report every lens
  result against a converged layer as well; the redirection is about a quarter lower there, and the front
  and dose values, computed with the paper's layer, should be read with that factor. No perfectly matched
  layer was used.
\item \textbf{Discretization.} Lens metrics change by at most about $7\%$ under $2\times$ mesh refinement. The
  panel's sensor and payload ratios change by at most $\WPanMeshSenDev\%$ on a finer mesh, its rim ratios by up to
  $\WPanMeshRimDevAbs$ in absolute terms. The optimized cloak is mesh-tuned, and its fixed-design refinement series
  has not converged.
\item \textbf{Optimality.} The optimizations are local (MMA). The dose and front studies use multiple starts,
  and their values are best designs found, i.e.\ lower bounds on what is attainable.
\item \textbf{Ray picture.} The ray prediction is single-pass and qP-only. It reproduces the size of the
  redirection but not its frequency dependence, which comes from reflections and interference.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Outlook}
The channeling picture suggests where orientation design should go next: routing problems around
prescribed holes and joints, where tows must wrap obstacles within the curvature limit; multi-objective
layouts that trade wave routing against stiffness; combined topology and toolpath design, in which voids are
cut rather than softened; and ray-based design, in which \eqref{eq:rays} replaces the wave solver in early
design stages.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
In a CFRP lamina the energy of quasi-longitudinal waves is skewed toward the fibers, by up to
$\WSkewMax^\circ$, and $\WSTwentyCFRP\%$ of wave normals carry their energy within $20^\circ$ of them. A steered fiber
field therefore acts as a field of energy streamlines wherever it turns slowly on the wavelength scale: the
flux follows the fibers, and rays traced through the fibers predict the redirection. An orientation-only lens
redirects $\WBandConvMin$--$\WBandConvMax\times$ more energy into its target over the tested band once the absorbing
layer is converged, and routes several times more of the injected power there. Its much larger
single-frequency gain is a resonance of the finite plate. The redirection vanishes for isotropic material,
grows with anisotropy, and survives tow-curvature limits down to a minimum radius of about $0.7$ along-fiber
wavelengths, which sets the size and frequency at which a given placement process can use it. A full cloak
cancels the far field strongly only at its design frequency and on its design mesh. Band-averaged design turns
channeling into multifunctional layouts whose performance holds across the operating band.
