\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
All results use the CFRP lamina of Sec.~\ref{sec:model} unless stated, an $x$-directed drive at the
left edge (a plane-wave line source for the lens and the cloak, a Gaussian beam for the panel,
Table~\ref{tab:panelparams}), and the absorbing sponge on the other three sides. Only the fiber
orientation is designed. Material, thickness and mass are identical to the straight-fiber baseline.

\subsection{The lens: what the optimizer buys, and what it does not (W1, W2)}\label{sec:lens}
On a $4\times3$ plate at $\omega=40$ (Table~\ref{tab:params}), maximizing the energy in a target box
(dashed square in Fig.~\ref{fig:lens}) yields a mirror-symmetric field of tows bending toward the target
near the right end of the plate. Its single-frequency target-energy gain against straight fibers is
$G=\WGainForty\times$. The decomposition \eqref{eq:gainsplit} shows what this number contains
(Fig.~\ref{fig:lensband}, Table~\ref{tab:lensband}).

\paragraph{The benchmark: a converged absorbing layer.} A finite plate with a reflecting boundary holds
standing waves, and reflections from the layer feed the target. We therefore lead with an absorbing
layer whose reflection is converged. The paper's layer (width 0.6) reflects $|R|\approx\WReflMin$--$\WReflMax$
of the along-fiber qP wave. Widening it outward, with the interior unchanged, gives $|R|\le\WReflOne$,
$\WReflTwo$ and $\WReflThree$ for widths 1.2, 1.8 and 2.4 (\texttt{wt\_r1\_lens.py absorber}). Over that
series the band-averaged redirection, both of the paper's lens and of a lens re-optimized with the
wider layer, is $\WBandConvMin$--$\WBandConvMax\times$ and no longer changes with $|R|$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:lensband}b). This is the absorber-independent redirection of the lens. With the paper's
layer the same lens gives $\WRedirBandMean\times$: reflections inflate the redirection by about a third.
The band average $\langle G_\varphi\rangle_{\rm band}$ is defined throughout as the mean of $G_\varphi$ at
$\omega\in\{35,40,45,50\}$.

\paragraph{Redirection, power, and growth.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Redirection is broadband over the tested wavelengths.} The target's share of the interior
  energy rises from $\WPhiZero\%$ to $\WPhiOne\%$ at $\omega=40$ ($G_\varphi=\WRedirForty\times$; with the wider
  layer, re-optimized, $\WAbsP\times$). Sweeping the drive frequency on the \emph{fixed} design,
  $G_\varphi\ge\WRedirBandMin\times$ for every $\omega\in[37.5,60]$ ($\ge\WAbsMinFortySixty\times$ over
  $[40,60]$ with the wider layer). At $\omega=20$ and $25$ the redirection collapses and even reverses
  ($G_\varphi<1$): there the along-fiber wavelength ($\lambda_\parallel\ge2.3$) exceeds the length over which
  the fibers turn, and the ray picture no longer applies. ``Broadband'' therefore means
  $\lambda_\parallel\lesssim1.5$ for this lens, not every frequency.
\item \textbf{Power is routed, not only stored.} The share of the injected power that the target absorbs
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:power}) rises from $\WPowPaperPiZero\%$ to $\WPowPaperPiOne\%$ at $\omega=40$, and its
  ratio to straight fibers is $\WPowPaperMin$--$\WPowPaperMax\times$ over $\omega\in[35,50]$
  ($\WPowAbsMin$--$\WPowAbsMax\times$ for the re-optimized lens with the wider layer;
  Fig.~\ref{fig:lensband}d). This ratio is also raised near the resonance, where the plate dissipates a larger
  fraction of the injected power structurally rather than in the sponge. As a share of the structural loss
  alone, the target's power rises $\WPowNormPaperMin$--$\WPowNormPaperMax\times$ ($\WPowNormAbsMin$--$\WPowNormAbsMax\times$).
  Stored-energy share and absorbed-power share agree in sign and order of magnitude at every frequency
  tested, so the redirection is a redirection of power flow, not only of standing energy.
\item \textbf{The excess gain is whole-field growth, consistent with resonance.} The interior field grows
  $\WGrowthForty\times$ at $\omega=40$ ($\WAbsGr\times$ with the wider layer, where $G=\WAbsG\times=\WAbsP\times$
  redirection $\times\,\WAbsGr\times$ growth). A fine sweep ($\Delta\omega=0.25$) shows a single peak of the
  growth, $\WFinePeak\times$ at $\omega=\WFinePeakOm$, with a half-power width of $\WFineWidth$, i.e.\ a
  quality factor $\approx\WFineQ$, consistent with the plate's loss factor $\eta=0.02$ ($Q=50$) plus the
  sponge loss. Across the peak $G_\varphi$ varies smoothly ($\WFineGpMin$--$\WFineGpMax$, with a broad maximum near
  $\omega\approx41.4$) and has no peak at the resonance.
  The growth also scales as $1/\eta$ while $G_\varphi$ does not (Appendix~\ref{app:verify}). At $\omega=37.5$
  and $42.5$, only $\pm6\%$ away, $G$ falls to $\WGainMinusFive\times$ and $\WGainPlusFive\times$.
\end{itemize}
A large single-frequency focus gain is therefore not by itself evidence of steering. It mostly
measures how well the optimizer tuned a standing wave. The transferable result is the redirection.

For W1, setting the lamina isotropic makes $G=G_\varphi=1$ to machine precision, and every orientation
gradient vanishes identically (Remark~\ref{rem:iso}; Sec.~\ref{sec:dose}).

\subsection{Mechanism: the energy flux follows the fibers (W3)}\label{sec:flux}
We compute the time-averaged energy flux \eqref{eq:flux} cell by cell and its angle to the local fiber
director (Fig.~\ref{fig:flux}). Cells whose flux is below $5\%$ of the maximum are excluded: there the
direction is ill-defined (standing-wave nodes), and they carry little power. The remaining
$\WFluxStrongFrac\%$ of the interior carries the energy transport.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Optimized lens.} The $|\bm P|$-weighted mean deviation of the flux from the fibers is
  $\WFluxDevMasked^\circ$ at $\omega=40$ ($\WFluxDev^\circ$ without the mask), and $\WFluxShareMasked\%$ of the
  flux runs within $20^\circ$ of them. The flux deviates $\WFluxDevX^\circ$ on average from the drive
  direction $x$. Across the band the (masked) deviation is $\WFluxDevMThirtyFive^\circ$, $\WFluxDevMFortyFive^\circ$
  and $\WFluxDevMFifty^\circ$ at $\omega=35,45,50$.
\item \textbf{Where channeling fails.} The deviation map (Fig.~\ref{fig:flux}b) is small almost everywhere
  and large in one place: just behind the target, where the fibers converge sharply. Binned by how fast
  the fibers turn (Fig.~\ref{fig:flux}d), the mean deviation stays at $2$--$4^\circ$ while the fibers turn
  by less than about $4$\,rad per along-fiber wavelength, and rises to $12$--$24^\circ$ beyond. This is an
  empirical threshold for the ray picture of Sec.~\ref{sec:theory} (it rests mainly on the two highest bins),
  more permissive than a strict slow-variation (WKB) condition. The lens's sharpest turning has a
  gradient length $L_\theta=0.15$, a tenth of $\lambda_\parallel$ (Table~\ref{tab:scales}).
\item \textbf{qP content.} As a proxy for the qP share, $\WPolLens\%$ of the lens's interior kinetic energy is
  polarized along the local fibers ($\WPolStraight\%$ with straight fibers; $\WPolMin$--$\WPolMax\%$ across the band). In CFRP the qP
  polarization lies within a few degrees of the fiber for most wave normals, so this is the qP-like
  share (an upper bound, since qS waves crossing the fibers are also polarized along them). The rest is qS
  motion and mode conversion, which channeling does not describe.
\item \textbf{Rays predict the redirection.} Tracing qP rays \eqref{eq:rays} through the fiber field alone
  (\texttt{wt\_r1\_rays.py}; Fig.~\ref{fig:lens}c) gives a target share of $\WRayShareLens\%$ against
  $\WRayShareStraight\%$ for straight fibers. That is $G_{\rm ray}=\WRayPaper\times$ for the paper's lens and
  $\WRayAbs\times$ for the re-optimized one, against $\WBandConvMin$--$\WBandConvMax\times$ from the full field
  with a converged absorber. The ray picture, which has no reflections, interference or resonance,
  accounts for the measured redirection to within about $20\%$.
\item \textbf{Control: fields that were never optimized.} Three random smooth CS-RBF fields (same support
  grid as the lens, support values drawn from $\mathcal N(0,0.5\,\mathrm{rad})$) and one analytic field,
  $\theta=0.6\sin(\pi x/2)\sin(2\pi y/3)$, give flux--fiber deviations of only $\WCtrlDevMin$--$\WCtrlDevMax^\circ$
  at $\omega=40$ and $50$, against $\WCtrlXMin$--$\WCtrlXMax^\circ$ from the drive direction
  (Appendix~\ref{app:verify}). Their fields turn more gently than the lens's, which is why they channel
  more tightly.
\end{itemize}
The energy goes where the fibers point, at every frequency tested and whether or not the field was
optimized, wherever the fibers turn slowly on the wavelength scale. Channeling is imposed by the lamina;
the optimizer only chooses where the fibers point. This is why the redirection is broadband.

\subsection{How the control scales with anisotropy (W4)}\label{sec:dose}
Along the family $\mathbf Q(s)=\mathbf Q_{\rm iso}+s(\mathbf Q_f-\mathbf Q_{\rm iso})$, which holds the isotropic invariants
and scales the anisotropic ones by $s$, we re-optimize the lens for each material from straight fibers
and from three random starts (\texttt{wt\_r1\_dose\_ms.py}), and evaluate every design with both the
paper's layer and the wider one (Table~\ref{tab:dose}, Fig.~\ref{fig:limitsW}a). The spread over starts is up
to $\WDoseMSmaxSpread$ in $\langle G_\varphi\rangle$. At $E_{f1}/E_{f2}=2.9$, for example, the four starts give
$\WDoseHalfMin$--$\WDoseHalfMax$, and the straight-start run is the highest of them. The
best of four rises monotonically with anisotropy under both absorbing layers: from exactly $1$ at
isotropy to $\WDoseBestQuarterAbs\times$ at $E_{f1}/E_{f2}=1.7$, $\WDoseBestCFRPAbs\times$ for CFRP and
$\WDoseBestMaxAbs\times$ at $E_{f1}/E_{f2}=32$ with the wider layer ($\WDoseBestCFRP\times$ for CFRP with the
paper's). The single-frequency gain $G$ along the same family is \emph{non-monotone} ($\WDoseGThree\times$ at
$E_{f1}/E_{f2}=2.9$ but $\WDoseGFive\times$ at $5.4$), because it is dominated by whichever resonance each
optimization tunes. W4 holds for the best design found. The detailed shape of the curve is within the
spread of the optimizer.

\subsection{Tow-curvature limits (W5)}\label{sec:front}
We bound the tow curvature $|\zeta|\le\zeta_{\rm all}=1/R$, keeping the lens's own mesh, frequency and
parametrization (Table~\ref{tab:front}, Fig.~\ref{fig:limitsW}b), and state $R$ in along-fiber wavelengths
$\lambda_\parallel=1.42$ (Table~\ref{tab:scales}).
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Plateau.} The best band-averaged redirection stays at $\WFrontOne$--$\WFrontUnc\times$ for every
  limit down to $\zeta_{\rm all}=1.0$, i.e.\ a minimum tow radius $R=1.0\approx0.7\lambda_\parallel$ (against the
  cross-fiber qP wavelength $0.37$ the same radius is $2.7$ wavelengths). We use the along-fiber value
  because the channeled energy travels along the fibers. The front lies at the edge of the slowly
  turning regime assumed by the ray argument, so we read it as an empirical scale rather than a ray
  prediction.
\item \textbf{Decline.} Beyond that radius the best design found falls gradually: $\WFrontEight\times$ at
  $R=1.25$, $\WFrontSix\times$ at $R=1.67$ ($\approx1.2\lambda_\parallel$), $\WFrontFourFive\times$ at $R=2.2$ and
  $\WFrontTight\times$ at $R=\WFrontTightR$. Tows that cannot turn on the wavelength scale cannot converge the
  channeled energy on the target within the plate.
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{How the front is computed, and how robust it is.} The optimizations are local and strongly
start-dependent under a curvature limit. At each limit from $\zeta_{\rm all}=0.3$ to $1.5$ we therefore run
$\WFrontNStart$ starts ($\WFrontNDistinct$ of which lead to distinct designs): every earlier front design and
three random fields, each scaled until feasible (\texttt{lens\_\allowbreak front\_\allowbreak robust.py}). The
spread is large (at $\zeta_{\rm all}=1.0$ from $\WFrontOneMin\times$ to $\WFrontOne\times$; shaded bars in
Fig.~\ref{fig:limitsW}b), so single runs can stall far below what the limit permits. A design whose actual
curvature satisfies a limit is feasible for that limit and for every looser one, so Table~\ref{tab:front}
reports, at each limit, the best band-averaged redirection over \emph{all} computed designs that satisfy
it; for $\zeta_{\rm all}=\infty$ every design counts. These values are lower bounds on what each limit allows.
They are with the paper's absorbing layer; by Sec.~\ref{sec:lens} the absorber-independent values are about
a quarter lower, and the shape of the front is not affected.

\subsection{The cloak: what bounded anisotropy can and cannot do (W5)}\label{sec:cloak}
The cloak is a harder and less robust problem than the lens, and we report it with that caveat. On an
$8\times5$ plate at $\omega=50$, a circular traction-free void of radius $R_V=0.55$ is cut conformally from the
mesh. Orientation may change only inside a shell $r<R_{\rm des}=1.5$ (exactly straight outside, enforced by a
smooth taper carried through the chain rule). The far-field mismatch to the void-free (incident) field is
minimized outside $R_C=1.7$, a region the design cannot touch.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{At the design frequency, on the design mesh.} The optimized shell reduces the mismatch
  $\WCloakPeak\times$. Fig.~\ref{fig:cloak}e--h shows what this means: the \emph{scattered} field
  $|\vu-\vu_{\rm inc}|$, on the same scale as the incident field, is nearly extinguished outside $R_C$ while it
  remains strong inside the shell, so the cancellation is a far-field effect, not a quiet plate. The
  transformation-cloak prescription \cite{pendry2006,norris2008}, imposed as fiber \emph{directions} on our
  fixed-contrast lamina, gives $\WCloakAnalytic\times$, because what it actually requires is a diverging
  \emph{magnitude} of anisotropy, which no lamina has. Its fiber curvature is also singular at the rim.
\item \textbf{Mesh sensitivity of a fixed design.} An interference cancellation is sensitive to the
  discrete dispersion of the mesh it was tuned on. Transferred unchanged to conforming meshes with
  $h=\WCSerHA$, $\WCSerHB$, $\WCSerHC$ and $\WCSerHD$ ($\WCSerNA$ to $\WCSerND$ cells), the same design gives
  $\WCSerA$, $\WCSerB$, $\WCSerC$ and $\WCSerD\times$. The series has not converged. The curvature-limited
  design changes less ($\WCSerCurvA\to\WCSerCurvD\times$) and the transformation directions hardly at all
  ($\WCSerTrA\to\WCSerTrD\times$). Re-optimized on the $h=0.03$ mesh (warm start, 80 iterations), the shell
  reaches $\WCloakFineReopt\times$ and is just as narrowband ($\WCloakFineMinus\times$ and $\WCloakFinePlus\times$ at
  $\pm5\%$). The capability persists under refinement, while any one optimized design is tuned to its
  discretization. Its converged value is therefore not established, and we give the cloak correspondingly
  less weight than the lens.
\item \textbf{Absorbing layer.} With a layer twice as wide (domain enlarged outward, interior unchanged) the
  three designs give $\WCSpOpt$, $\WCSpCurv$ and $\WCSpTr\times$ (\texttt{wt\_r1\_cloak.py sponge}), so the
  cancellation is not an artifact of reflections from the layer.
\item \textbf{Across frequency.} As W5 predicts, the optimized cloak is narrowband (Table~\ref{tab:cloakband}):
  $\WCloakMinus\times$ at $\omega=47.5$, $\WCloakPlus\times$ at $52.5$, and $\WCloakForty\times$ at $40$. There it is
  outperformed by the transformation-direction field ($\WCloakAnalyticForty\times$).
\item \textbf{Under a tow-curvature limit.} With the curvature bounded at the void's own scale,
  $\zeta_{\rm all}=1/R_V$, the cloak reaches $\WCloakCurv\times$ at the design frequency. This is less peaked but
  also less narrowband ($\WCloakCurvMinus$--$\WCloakCurvPlus\times$ over $\pm5\%$, and at least $\WCloakCurvBandMin\times$ over
  $\omega\in[40,60]$), and less mesh-sensitive.
\end{itemize}
Bounded-anisotropy cloaking is therefore real but tuned, to its frequency and to its mesh. Orientation
fields that respect the tow-curvature limit cancel a scatterer's far field moderately and robustly; strong
cancellation is narrowband and discretization-specific.

\subsection{As built: tow spreading and defects}\label{sec:asbuilt}
The designs are idealized fields with one angle per point, constant thickness and full coverage. Laying
the lens with constant-width tows along its paths spreads and converges the tows at the rate
$\nabla\!\cdot\bm n$, with $\bm n=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. A course that starts fully covering its inflow edge reaches
$\vx$ with relative density $\tau=\exp(-\int\nabla\!\cdot\bm n\,\mathrm{d}\ell)$, integrated back along the path. For the
lens, $\tau$ spans $\WTauMin$ to $\WTauMax$, so the field cannot be laid with uncut courses and needs tow cut/add.
With cut/add inside courses of $N$ tows, the coverage mismatch averages half a tow at a course boundary. As in
the defect-layer method \cite{fayazbakhsh2013}, we model a defect area fraction
$f_d=\tfrac{1}{2N}\min(1,|\ln\tau|/\ln(1+1/N))$ as resin gaps (stiffness and density reduced toward those of epoxy in proportion to $f_d$, keeping the lamina's anisotropy) or as overlaps
(thickness $1+f_d$; \texttt{wt\_r1\_lens.py asbuilt}). For $N=4$, $8$ and $16$ the band-averaged redirection
stays at $\WAsBuiltMin$--$\WAsBuiltMax\times$, against $\WRedirBandMean\times$ for the idealized field, with the
paper's layer. Wave redirection is insensitive to such defects because it depends on the fiber
\emph{direction}, which the defects do not change. The defect model, in particular its cap
$f_d\le1/(2N)$, is an assumption, and tow width, process defects and multi-ply laminates are not
resolved.

\subsection{Design practice: a band-robust multifunctional panel}\label{sec:panel}
The preceding results imply two design rules. Optimize over a frequency band rather than at one
frequency, so that the optimizer cannot buy performance with a resonance. Ask the fiber field to route
energy, which channeling does broadband. A bolted $7\times4$ CFRP panel with four conforming fastener holes
is designed this way. A single mirror-symmetric, curvature-feasible field ($\max|\zeta|=\WPanZmax\le1/R_{\rm
tow}$), optimized over $\WPanNF$ frequencies in $[\WPanBLo,\WPanBHi]$, does four things at the band center
(Fig.~\ref{fig:panel}; parameters in Table~\ref{tab:panelparams}):
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item it routes the incident vibration onto a sensor patch ($\WPanRoute\times$);
\item it shields a payload core to $\WPanShield\times$ its straight-fiber energy;
\item it keeps the four fatigue-critical fastener rims at $\WPanProt\times$;
\item it stiffens the bolted mount $\WPanStiff\times$ at no added mass (stiffness is an explicit, weighted term of
  the objective).
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Across the band, and why.} Averaged over the design band $[44,56]$ the sensor receives
$\WPanSenMean\times$, the payload $\WPanPayMean\times$ and the rims $\WPanRimMean\times$ their straight-fiber energies
(Fig.~\ref{fig:panel}f); the worst cases in the band are $\WPanBandSenMin\times$ (sensor),
$\WPanBandPayMax\times$ (payload) and $\WPanRimWorst\times$ (rims). Split as for the lens, the sensor's gain is
redirection, not resonance: its share of the interior energy is $\WPanShareMin$--$\WPanShareMax\times$ that with
straight fibers across the band, while the whole interior field is \emph{smaller}, $\WPanGrowthMin$--$\WPanGrowthMax\times$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:panel}e). A design optimized at the band center alone, with the same settings, routes slightly
better but lets the rim protection degrade toward the lower band edge, to $\WPanToneRimWorst\times$ the
straight-fiber energy (band mean $\WPanToneRimMean\times$). The band objective buys robust rim protection at a
small cost in routing. The objective, a single signed region weight with logarithmic delivery and
straight-fiber normalization, is given in Appendix~\ref{app:panelobj}.
